Cognitive Accessibility Guide

A website can be hard to use when instructions are confusing, pages are crowded, or buttons keep moving. Cognitive accessibility helps people with difficulties in attention, memory, reading, or processing information, and clear design helps everyone.

Write in plain language

Use familiar words and short sentences. Explain technical terms when they are needed.

Organize content into sections

Headings, short paragraphs, and logical steps make information easier to follow.

Keep navigation consistent

Use familiar menus and keep important controls in predictable places.

Explain tasks clearly

Tell users what to do, one step at a time.

Make errors easy to fix

Instead of “Invalid input,” explain what went wrong and how to correct it.

Everyday example

“Please enter a valid email address, such as name@example.com” helps more than a form that only says “Error.”

Key takeaway

Cognitive accessibility helps people understand information and finish tasks with less confusion.
